01Quick start

Three minutes from address to answer. The golden rule throughout: Heliotrope only knows about shade-casters you trace — the app can see your yard, but you must tell it how tall things are.

Search your address The map flies to a satellite view of your actual yard and every sunrise, shadow and sun-hour locks to your latitude. Use the ◎ button to jump to your current location.

Trace the shade-casters first Before drawing a single bed, outline what blocks light: trees (T) with their real height and canopy, buildings (U), fences and walls (F). Heights are set in the inspector after placing — eyeball a two-story house at ~20 ft, a mature maple at 40–60 ft.

Draw beds where the light is Switch on Best sun, find the gold, trace beds (B) over it, then open the plant library (P) and plant. The Garden Ledger will warn you if anything is starved of light, crowded, or next to a bad companion.

02The tool rail

Left side of the screen, top to bottom. Every tool has a one-key shortcut.

➤Select & move V

Click anything to inspect it; drag to move it. Selected beds and buildings grow white corner handles — drag a handle to reshape. Trees get a radius handle on the canopy edge.

Click a warning in the Garden Ledger to fly straight to the offending plant.

▦Garden bed B

Click the corners of the bed over the soil you can see; close with Enter, a double-click, or by clicking the first corner again. Area in ft² updates live.

Name beds in the inspector — "Tomato row", "Shade corner" — the labels render on the map.

🌳Tree T

One click plants it. Set height, canopy radius, and — crucially — deciduous vs. evergreen. Deciduous trees barely block winter sun once leafless; evergreens shade year-round.

Match the canopy circle to the tree's real crown in the imagery — it's usually bigger than you think.

⌂Building U

Trace the footprint, set the height. Buildings cast full extruded shadows — the north side of a two-story house is a serious shade zone.

Trace the neighbor's house and trees too. Their shade doesn't respect property lines.

╫Fence / wall F

Click along the line, double-click to finish, set the height. A 6 ft privacy fence shades a surprising strip — morning on its west side, evening on its east.

Fences are where cool-season greens hide from brutal afternoon sun.

🌱Plant P

Opens the 40-plant library — search or filter by category, click a plant to arm it, then click the map to place. Spread circles show mature size; a ! badge appears on any plant not getting the light it needs.

Select any plant to read spacing, sowing windows, companions and a growing tip.

⌖Light probe L

Click any spot to chart its personal day of sunlight. Covered in depth below — this is the most precise instrument in the app.

The probe is draggable with Select and survives reloads.

◇Erase E & Undo ⌘Z

Erase removes whatever you click. Undo walks back the last 40 changes. ✕ Clear in the top bar wipes the whole design (undoable this session).

Duplicate in the inspector clones any object — fastest way to plant a row of anything.

03The five sun instruments

Bottom dock, right-hand toggles. Each answers a different question — the craft is knowing which one to reach for.

☀ Shadows — "what does the yard look like right now?"

Live shadows drawn on the imagery for the exact date and time on the sliders. Drag the time slider or press ▶ (or Space) to animate the whole day in a few seconds — dawn tint, sweeping shadows, golden hour, dusk. The sun dial (bottom right) shows the sun's compass position, altitude, and the full arc it will travel today.

▤ Sun map — "how many hours does every square foot get?"

The full analysis: the day is simulated at 48 sun positions and every ~half-meter cell is scored by hours of direct sun, painted dark (shade) through gold (full sun). Hover anywhere to read the exact hours at the cursor in the legend.

The map follows the time slider — it shows sun banked so far by the chosen hour. Press ▶ and watch the yard fill with light through the day; drag the slider to the evening (or read the legend's "day total" note) for the full-day figure, which is what the plant warnings use. The thresholds that matter are marked:

0h — deep shade4h — part sun6h — full sun12h+

◉ Best sun — "just show me the good spots"

Same analysis, opposite presentation: the imagery stays clean and only the strong light is painted — glowing gold where a spot earns 6h+ of full sun, faint amber for the 4–6h part-sun band, nothing below. When you're zoomed to yard scale it analyzes everything on screen and follows as you pan — so you can scout an empty yard before drawing anything at all.

◠ Trails — "where will the shadow be at 9am? At 3pm?"

The whole day's shadow choreography in a single view. Every obstacle draws an outline of where its shade reaches at each hour — cool blue mornings through warm orange evenings, labeled 6a … 12p … 6p. A tree's trails fan out like a sundial around it: long west-pointing morning ellipses, a tight ring at noon, long east-pointing evening ones.

⌖ Light probe — "tell me about this exact spot"

Click a spot with the probe tool and get its minute-by-minute sun timeline for the chosen date: a sunrise-to-sunset strip (gold = direct sun, slate = shaded, amber = filtered winter canopy), total direct-sun hours with a full/part/shade verdict, and — the key part — every shaded stretch listed with its cause:

The probe recomputes instantly when you scrub the date slider — hold it on a spot and drag through the year to watch a "full sun" verdict collapse to "part sun" in October.

04Recipes

Worked examples that chain the instruments together. Each one is a real planning question answered in a few minutes.

Recipe № 1 · Before building anything

Scout the sunniest ground in an empty yard

  1. Search your address and zoom until your whole yard fills the screen.
  2. Trace every shade-caster: your house, the garage, the big trees — and the neighbor's. Get heights roughly right; they matter more than footprint precision.
  3. Set the date to a shoulder season — click Spring eqx. (Anywhere sunny at the equinox is glorious in June; June-only sun can fool you.)
  4. Toggle Best sun. The gold is your shortlist.
  5. Drop the probe (L) on the two or three best patches and compare their hour totals and shade windows before committing.
  6. Trace beds over the winner. Done — you've sited a garden the way landscape architects do.
Sun is the one thing you can't amend later. Soil can be fixed; a garage shadow can't.
Recipe № 2 · Full-sun crops

Place the tomatoes (and keep them happy)

  1. Turn on the Sun map on the summer solstice preset and hover the bed — you want 8h+ cells for tomatoes, peppers, squash.
  2. Plant tall crops — corn, sunflowers, trellised peas — on the bed's north edge. Toggle Trails to prove it: their shadow ellipses should sweep over the path, not the bed.
  3. Arm the tomato in the library and plant; leave the spread circles just barely kissing. The ledger flags crowding at a glance.
  4. Check the tomato's card: basil, marigold and carrot are friends — tuck them into the gaps. Keep potatoes and fennel in another bed entirely.
  5. Scrub the date to Fall eqx with the plants selected — if your tomato drops below 6h in September, that's your cue for an early-ripening variety.
A ⚠ badge on a plant means the analysis says it's light-starved on this date — the fix is moving the plant, raising the canopy, or changing the crop.
Recipe № 3 · The shady corner

Turn the dark zone under a tree into a garden

  1. Toggle Trails around your big tree and find the ground its ellipses cover most of the day — that's the deep-shade core.
  2. Probe it. Under 4h? That's hosta and ostrich fern country — both in the library, both flagged "Shade ok".
  3. Probe the fringe where morning ellipses reach but afternoon ones don't: 4–6h of gentle morning light is perfect for lettuce, spinach, mint, cilantro — cool-season greens that bolt in full afternoon sun.
  4. Plant the fringe with greens and the core with shade perennials. The same tree that ruins tomatoes just gave you a salad bar.
"Shade" is really two habitats: morning-lit fringe and true deep shade. The trails view is the only instrument that separates them by time of day.
Recipe № 4 · Seasons

The four-season audit (and the deciduous trick)

  1. With your design done, click the four season presets in order: Spring eqx → Summer sol → Fall eqx → Winter sol, watching the Sun map and ledger each time.
  2. Notice the deciduous trees: their canopy goes dashed in winter and nearly stops blocking sun. Ground that's shaded all summer may be winter-bright — ideal for garlic (planted October, up before the leaves return) and spring bulbs.
  3. Evergreens never let up. Anything within their trails needs true shade tolerance year-round.
  4. Winter-sun check for structures: probe your greenhouse or cold-frame spot on Winter sol — December's 9h day with a low sun makes building shadows three times longer than June's.
The sun drops ~47° between solstices. A yard is four different yards a year; the presets let you audit all four in under a minute.
Recipe № 5 · Beyond the vegetables

Site a patio, greenhouse, or reading bench

  1. For an evening patio: turn on Trails and find ground the warm-orange 5p/6p ellipses never touch — then probe it and confirm the strip stays gold past 5pm.
  2. For a greenhouse: you want winter sun above all. Winter sol preset + Best sun, and take the gold that sits clear of building shadows.
  3. For a shade bench: reverse the search — you want the 2p–4p ellipses. The east side of a big deciduous tree gives morning sun and afternoon refuge.
  4. Drag the time slider through the day with Shadows on for the final gut-check — watching your future patio fall into shade at 4:30pm is worth a thousand heat maps.
Every "where should X go" question is a sunlight question wearing different clothes — the same five instruments answer all of them.
Recipe № 6 · Housekeeping

Save, share, and iterate designs

  1. Everything auto-saves to this browser — close the tab and pick up where you left off.
  2. ⇩ Export downloads the whole design as JSON; ⇧ Import restores it. Trade files with the other gardener in your household and argue with data.
  3. Duplicate a bed, drag the copy elsewhere, and compare probes: an A/B test for garden plots.
  4. Made a mess? ⌘Z undoes the last 40 moves.
Exported files are plain JSON — version them in git if you're that kind of gardener. (You're reading a field guide for a satellite sun simulator. You are.)

06What to trust (and what to check outside)

Trust the geometry. Sun positions come from the standard solar ephemeris and are accurate to a fraction of a degree; shadow lengths and directions follow directly. Sunrise, sunset and day length are exact for your latitude.

Remember the model's edges. It's a clear-sky model — clouds, fog and your climate's marine layer are yours to factor in. Times are approximate local solar time (no DST). Terrain is flat: a yard on a north slope gets less than the map shows. And the golden rule one last time: untraced objects cast no shade — if the gold covers a forest you didn't outline, the forest wins in real life.

Calibrate once. Pick a shadow you can see in the satellite photo itself (most imagery is shot mid-morning). Set the time slider until the drawn shadows of your traced objects line up with the photographed ones — if they agree, every other hour of the simulation is on rails.
